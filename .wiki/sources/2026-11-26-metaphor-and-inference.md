# What the listener takes from a comparison

Ortony, Vondruska, Foss and Jones found evidence that the direction of a comparison matters. A property prominent in the source can bring a less obvious property of the target to attention. This account concerns interpretation. It does not establish that every intelligible comparison sounds natural. [Salience, similes, and the asymmetry of similarity (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(85)90047-6).

Bowdle and Gentner found that novel figurative expressions tended to invite comparison. With conventional use, a word can evoke an established figurative category. Their English-language studies do not measure Korean audience reactions. [The Career of Metaphor (2005)](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/BowdleGentner05.pdf).

The Korean dictionary example 손이 크다 applied to a host foregrounds generosity or abundance in the hospitality context. It illustrates an established reading. It gives us no reason to assume that a newly devised Go comparison will be read the same way. See [[2026-11-26-korean-situated-speech]].

In the Go pilot, 마음에 걸립니다 invites the viewer to wonder whether another move could have saved the stone. The right-hand move answers that question. The next line switches attention to the unchanged record, although neither player had reason to expect the record to change. The Swift proposal concerns a plausible competing expectation: the caller might see a value written back, as with inout. This diagnosis is an editorial inference. No viewer test has taken place. [SE-0003](https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/blob/main/proposals/0003-remove-var-parameters.md).

Proposed listening check: stop before the transition and ask a viewer unfamiliar with the script what question the scene has raised. Record their own words before explaining the connection.
